# Sageloom — Inside the Industry

## 1. Overview

**Inside the Industry** is a recurring Sageloom learning initiative designed to give students structured exposure to how real companies operate at the intersection of biology, bioinformatics, technology, data, AI, and business.

Each cycle focuses on one company. Students investigate the company using a common analytical framework and produce two complementary outputs:

1. **Blog Post (Long-Form):** A comprehensive, 1,200–2,000 word analysis published on the Sageloom website blog. This is the canonical version containing the full depth of the research — data landscape, pipeline walkthrough, teams and roles, skills map, industry context, and critical analysis.
2. **LinkedIn Post (Short-Form):** A 400–600 word highlight reel posted on the Sageloom LinkedIn page. This captures the most attention-grabbing insights and links back to the full blog post, driving traffic to the website.

The primary objective is **learning and industry understanding**, not company promotion.

## 2. Objectives

- Connect academic concepts with real-world industry applications.
- Help students understand how scientific problems become products, services, or platforms.
- Develop the ability to research companies using reliable public information.
- Help students understand the relationship between biology, data, computation, software, AI, and business.
- Expose students to different technical and non-technical roles within an organization.
- Help students identify skills relevant to specific industry domains and career paths.
- Develop technical communication and information-synthesis skills.
- Build a collection of structured company case studies that can be reused for learning.

## 3. Core Approach

Each company is examined through a consistent sequence:

**Problem → Science/Biology → Data → Technology → Workflow → Product → Teams → Business → Skills → Careers**

The framework provides consistency while allowing optional sections to be explored when they are particularly relevant to a company.

The analysis should focus on **how the different parts connect**, rather than simply describing what the company does.

## 4. Expected Student Outcomes

Through repeated participation, students should develop the ability to:

- Investigate an unfamiliar company independently.
- Identify the scientific and technical problems a company addresses.
- Trace how data moves through a real-world system.
- Recognize the technologies and skills used across different industry functions.
- Distinguish documented facts from interpretation or inference.
- Evaluate information from multiple public sources.
- Communicate complex technical information clearly.
- Compare different companies and identify common industry patterns.
- Translate industry observations into personal learning goals.

## 5. Faculty / Mentor Role

Faculty and mentors provide direction, context, and quality control rather than doing the investigation for the students.

Their role may include:

- Helping students frame useful research questions.
- Correcting important scientific or technical misunderstandings.
- Reviewing key claims before publication.
- Encouraging evidence-based reasoning.
- Connecting the investigation to concepts taught in the curriculum.
- Helping students identify meaningful learning and career takeaways.

## 6. Suggested Weekly Workflow

1. Select a company and define the focus.
2. Assign the company investigation to students or a student group.
3. Collect information from credible public sources.
4. Map the company using the universal research template.
5. Identify documented facts, interpretations, and unresolved questions.
6. Review the analysis with a faculty member or mentor.
7. **Draft the blog post** using `Blog_Post_Template.md` — the full-length analysis for the website.
8. **Draft the LinkedIn post** using `LinkedIn_Post_Template.md` — the short-form highlights that link to the blog.
9. Submit both drafts together to the Board via `request_confirmation` for approval.
10. Upon approval: publish the blog post to the website, then publish the LinkedIn post with the blog link.
11. Discuss the key learning points with the wider student group.

## 7. Potential Challenges and Mitigation

| Challenge | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Analysis becomes a generic company profile | Use the fixed decomposition framework and focus on relationships between problem, technology, product, and people. |
| Students make unsupported claims | Require credible sources and distinguish documented information from inference. |
| The phrase “inner workings” suggests access to proprietary information | Frame the activity as analysis based on publicly available information. |
| Content becomes promotional | Focus on learning, technical choices, roles, limitations, and industry context rather than praising the company. |
| Research quality varies between students | Use a common template, minimum evidence standards, and faculty review. |
| Weekly work becomes too time-consuming | Keep the public output concise and time-box the research. Detailed analysis can remain as a learning artifact. |
| Only large or famous companies are selected | Include startups, research organizations, diagnostics, pharma, software, AI, infrastructure, and other relevant company types. |
| Posts become repetitive | Keep the core framework consistent while allowing optional technical, competitive, and critical-analysis sections. |
| Students rely heavily on AI-generated summaries | Require source verification and explanations in the students' own words. |

## 8. Quality Principles

1. **Evidence before assumptions.**
2. **Learning before marketing.**
3. **Why before what.**
4. **Public information only.**
5. **Clear distinction between fact and inference.**
6. **Technical depth appropriate to the students' level.**
7. **Industry breadth rather than company popularity.**
8. **The investigation is the learning activity; the blog post and LinkedIn post are outputs that serve different audiences at different depths.**

## 9. Long-Term Value

With repeated use, the initiative can develop into a growing collection of industry case studies.

Students can compare companies across dimensions such as:

- Scientific problem
- Data type
- Computational methods
- AI/ML usage
- Software and infrastructure
- Products
- Business models
- Organizational roles
- Required skills
- Career opportunities

This allows students to gradually develop a broader understanding of the industry rather than viewing bioinformatics as a collection of isolated tools or subjects.

## 10. Public Positioning

A suitable description of the initiative is:

> A recurring Sageloom learning initiative in which students deconstruct real companies to understand how science, data, technology, products, teams, and careers come together in the industry.

The work should be presented as an **evidence-based educational analysis using publicly available information**, rather than as an attempt to describe confidential or proprietary company processes.
